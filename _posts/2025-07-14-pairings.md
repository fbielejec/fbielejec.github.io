# <a name="intro"/> Introduction

In this post I will go over ELlliptic Curve pairings.
Pairing are often sold as the "moon math" component of the ZeroKnowledge protocols (or at least for the ones based on pairings).

What follows is that they are often treated as a black-box, simply because covering them in a sufficient detail would take a considerable amount of time, and in most cases is not strictly needed from the engineering perspective.

I this blog post will explain what the black box does, _alas_ with the engineer's perspective in mind, therefore keeping the mathematical details down to a required minimum and with numerical examples to help guide the understanding of how pairings are constructed.

Note that there is a substantial amount of subject matter knowledge assumed, a broad but not exhaustive list can be found in the [Prerequisites](#pre) section below.

# <a name="why"/> Why

Let $\mathbb{F}_p$ be a finite field of a characteristic $p$ (a prime number) and let $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ be an elliptic curve.
Points on $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$, together with the so called abstractly defined point-at-infinity $\mathcal{O}$, which serves as the identity element, form an additive, cyclic group, with the group law $\textcircled{+}$ being the elliptic curve point addition.

We have fast and efficient algorithms for point addition, as well as point-doubling (adding a point ot iotself), and there exists a class of elliptic curve, the so-called [Edwards curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwards_curve) where there is a closed-form solution for both adding two distinct points as well as point-doubling.

Repeated point addition leads to the notion of the Elliptic Curve scalar multiplication $[\cdot]$:

$[\cdot]G: \med Z_{r} \rightarrow E(\mathbb{F_p}); \quad n \mapsto [n]G$

where:

- $E(\mathbb{F_p})$ is an elliptic curve.
- $G$ is Generator of $E(\mathbb{F_p})$ (or some prime order cyclic subgroup $\langle G \rangle$ of $E(\mathbb{F_p})$) of order $r$.
- $[n]G=\underbrace{G + G \cdot \dots \cdot G}_{n \text{ times}}$.
- $[0]G=\mathcal{O}$

Elliptic curve scalar multiplication is an instantiation of the more general [Exponential Map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_map_(Lie_theory)).

It's inverse is the [Discrete Logarithm Map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_logarithm):

$log_{G}(\cdot): \langle G \rangle \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}_r; \quad P \mapsto log_{G}(P)=k$

where:
- $P=[k]G$.

For the curves used in cryptography (e.g., BN128, secp256k1), there is currently no efficient algorithm to compute $\log_G$ <sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup>).

Additionally scalar multiplication respects the Elliptic Curve Group Law:

$[x+y]G=[x]G {\footnotesize \textcircled{+}} [y]G$

This means that scalar multiplication is an additive homomorphism, that works from the integers under addition (mod $r$) to the elliptic curve group under eliptic curve point addition group law.

The combination of these two properties:
- the fact that we have an efficiently computable map from the domain (integers modulo $r$) to a codomain (elliptic curve points)
- that is practically impossible to reverse.

Allows us to "hide the computation in the exponent".

Imagine Alice knows two scalars $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}_r$ that sum to a third number $c=a+b$.
Alice want to prove she knows such $a,b,c$ to Bob, without ever revealing them.
She would publish $A=[a]G, B=[b]G$ and $C=[a+b]G$ and Bob would verify that A+B == C.

This works becasue:
$A {\footnotesize \textcircled{+}} B = [a]G {\footnotesize \textcircled{+}} [b]G \underbrace{=}_{\text{homomorphic property}} [a+b]G$

This corresponds to adding secrets without revealing them.
The example below should make it more concrete:

## Example:

```python
p=97
F = GF(p)
E = EllipticCurve(F, [2,3])

P = E.random_point()
while not P.order().is_prime():
  P = E.random_point()

r = P.order()

Zr=Integers(r)

phi = lambda n,P: n*P

a = Zr(1)
b = Zr(2)

lhs = phi((a+b),P)
rhs = phi(a,P) + phi(b,P)

print(f"phi({a}+{b})={lhs} == phi({a})+phi({b})={rhs}: {lhs == rhs}")
```

```bash
phi(1+0)=(30 : 0 : 1) == phi(1)+phi(0)=(30 : 0 : 1): True
```

Therefore we have a notion of additive homomorphism, but what we cannot do is perform a multiplicatively homomorphic operations, becasue we have no definition of multiplying points on the elliptic curve.

This is where pairing functions can be used: they allow us to perform a (very limited!) form of multiplicatively homomorphic operations.
